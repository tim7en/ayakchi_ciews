EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
 
Uzbekistan is entering a decisive decade in which climate change will increasingly be transmitted 
through water, reshaping agriculture, energy security, public health, and rural livelihoods. Rising 
temperatures, shrinking snow and glacier storage, more frequent heatwaves, and intensifying 
floods, mudflows, and droughts are already narrowing the margin for erro r in operating the Amu 
Darya and Syr Darya systems and protecting vulnerable communities. This report  supports the 
Government’s response by mapping the institutional, regulatory, and digital landscape for Climate 
Information and Early Warning Systems (CIEWS) and by proposing a “forecast -to-field” 
architecture that connects regional river-basin governance, national climate services, basi n 
operations, and last-mile decisions. 
 
Analysis of national observations, Uzbekistan’s Fourth National Communication 1 and Biennial 
Transparency Report,2 and recent scientific review confirms that the country has warmed faster  
than global averages, with statistically significant increases in annual mean temperature at most 
stations and particularly strong warming in lowland deserts. Heatwaves are more frequent and 
intense, driving excess mortality and a rising burden of heat-sensitive disease, while m udflows 
and landslides remain among the most damaging hazards in eastern and southern moun tain 
belts. Multi-model projections point to continued rapid warming, especially in summer, modest but 
important shifts in seasonal precipitation, shorter frost and snow seasons, and  greater 
hydrological variability in key runoff-formation zones. Even where annual precipi tation remains 
near current levels, the timing and form of runoff will change, increasing  both drought and flood 
risk and placing higher demands on reservoir storage, conveyance, and early warning. 
 
Uzbekistan confronts these risks with a strong reform platform. The Uzbeki stan-2030 Strategy,3 
the Strategy for Transition to a Green Economy, updated Nationally Det ermined Contribution 
(NDC) 3.0, 4 and the designation of 2025 as the “Year of Environmental Protection and Gree n 
Economy” anchors climate ambition in national policy. Institutionally, this agenda is now led by 
the newly established National Committee on Ecology and Climate Change (NCECC), formerly 
the Ministry of Ecology, Environmental Protection and Climate Change.5 NCECC reports directly 
to the President and collectively works as the center for climate information and coo rdination for 
CIEWS development along with the Centre of Hydrometeorological Service (Uzhydromet) and the 
National Framework for Climate Services (NFCS). Recent water- and agriculture-se ctor reforms 
include updated rules on water use, the creation of specialized Water Supply Services (WSS ), 
accelerated digitalization of water management, and the roll-out of the Agroplatforma /CropAgro 
ecosystem. These reforms create a more integrated, data-driven chain from clim ate signals to 
crop planning, water allocation, and sustainable rural financing. 
 
Regionally and nationally, CIEWS is embedded in relatively advanced water-governance  
architecture. The International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS) and the Interstate Commission 
for Water Coordination 6 (ICWC), supported by Basin Water Organizations (BWOs) and the 
Scientific-Information Center of ICWC, translate seasonal water-availability forecasts into shared

functioning as a fully impact- based, “forecast-to-action” system. Warning products are not yet 
anchored to binding, cross-ministerial trigger matrices that automatically translate thresholds into 
reservoir drawdowns, canal operations, crop-rotation adjustments, and social-protection or health 
measures. Sectoral bulletins and inter-agency risk assessments are largely ad hoc rather than 
issued on a fixed cadence with clear service expectations. Observation and communications 
redundancy remain uneven, particularly in high-risk mountain and rural areas. Long-term 
operation and maintenance funding is not securely ring-fenced. Data standards and identifiers  
are fragmented across hydrometeorological, water, agriculture, cadastre, and pump-mete r 
systems, complicating interoperability and limiting the reliability of satellite and normative fallback 
methods where meters are absent. At the last mile, the conversion of alerts into actionable farm-
level decisions depends heavily on WSSs and WUAs whose digital access, technical capaci ty, 
and inclusivity are variable, while capacity in impact modeling, GIS, and risk com munication at 
regional and district levels remains limited.

Based on national institutions and extensive field consultations in the cities of Fergana, A ndijan, 
and Namangan on detailed mapping of mandates, data flows, and decision pathways, this report 
recommends a phased modernization of Uzbekistan’s CIEWS. Key priorities are to: (i) formalize 
national, sector-specific trigger matrices that link Uzhydromet thresholds to standard ope rating 
procedures in MoWR, MES and MoA; (ii) create a unified, real-time multi-ha zard dashboard 
combining meteorological, hydrological, water-accounting, pump-meter and cadastral da ta; (iii) 
complete universal metering and anti-tamper enforcement while harmonizing identifiers and data 
schemas across platforms; (iv) secure predictable lifecycle financing for observation,  radar and 
communications infrastructure; (v) expand inclusive last-mile channels and training for W SS, 
WUAs and community actors; and (vi) embed forecast-triggered financing and contingency plans 
for drought, heat and floods. Piloting this “forecast -to-field” package, with clear per formance 
indicators, would enable iterative learning before national scale-up and deeper  integration with 
regional platforms. This will help Uzbekistan consolidate recent reforms into a coherent, climate-
smart CIEWS that protects lives and livelihoods, optimizes scarce water resources, and supports 
resilient green transition.

I. INTRODUCTION 
 
A. Background on Central Asia Climate 
 
1. Central Asia’s climate risks are intensifying, with impacts transmitted strongly through 
water into energy, agriculture/irrigation, public health, and watershed management. In t he Amu 
Darya–Syr Darya systems, reduced snow/ice storage, higher evapotranspiration and more 
frequent heat/drought events strain irrigation demand, reservoir operations and flood/mudflow risk 
management, especially in the lowlands. 
 
2. The region is highly vulnerable to climate change, with human-driven greenhouse ga ses 
dominating the region’s century-scale forcing. Observations indicate about 1.2 °C of warming over 
the past 70 years and roughly a 20% decline in snow depth—most pronounced in the mountains—
along with longer summers and more than 20 fewer icing days per year. Anal ysis of 442 
CMIP512/CMIP613 simulations show CMIP6 is generally warmer/wetter and more temperature-
sensitive than CMIP5. Under high-emission pathways (RCP8.5 14/SSP5-8.515) CMIP6 projects 
~6.1°C warming versus ~5.3°C in CMIP5, while precipitation changes are weaker and less  
consistent. Greater snow/glacier melt boosts near-glacier runoff and flood risk, an d 2–6 °C of 
regional warming by 2100 is plausible, >4 °C is very likely without mitigation, extending the glacier-
melt season. Model uncertainty grows in the far future, but both ensembles capture observed 
warming trends. Köppen classifications shift toward warmer types, with tundra/ice-cap zones in 
the Amu Darya and Syr Darya basins shrinking by >60%, implying major impacts on hydrol ogy 
and water management.

II. Country Climate Risk Context 
 
5. Uzbekistan is a lower-middle income, landlocked country located in the hea rt of Central 
Asia. Approximately 78.8% of its territory consists of plains, with almost 80% char acterized by 
flat, desert topography and 70% lying in the arid zone. While earlier assessments ranked 
Uzbekistan 24 th in global natural disaster hotspots, recent evaluations show the country faces 
significant climate risks with natural disasters affecting approximately 1.4 million people annually 
and causing nearly USD3 billion in losses.17 Although the country has developed its first National 
Framework for Hydrometeorological and Climate Services and submitted its inaugural Biennial 
Transparency Report under the Paris Agreement in December 2024, challenges remain in 
harmonizing early warning systems and achieving full coverage across vulnerable areas. 
 
A. Impact of Climate Change 
 
6. Indicated below are synthesized findings reported in Uzbekistan’s Fourth National 
Communication under the UNFCCC, 18 which compiles Uzhydromet’s long -term observations, 
national disaster statistics, and sectoral assessments. Quantitative values are drawn directly from 
the report, and its underlying station-level and model-based datasets. 
 
a. Temperature and precipitation.  Uzhydromet’s analysis for 1950– 2019 indicates a 
statistically significant rise in average annual air temperature throughout most  of 
Uzbekistan. Warming rates are estimated at approximately 0.2 –0.4°C per decade, with 
Karakalpakstan and other lowland desert regions exhibiting the most pronounced trends. 
Mountain areas warm more slowly but still show clear upward trends. Overall annu al 
precipitation shows no strong countrywide trend, but regional patterns are diverging, with 
slight increases in the north (Ustyurt and Aral Sea area) and declines in several southern 
and central regions. These changes already influence water availability, snow regimes, 
and runoff timing. Detailed station-level results are provided in Annex II (Table 1). 
 
b. Heat stress and health. Rising heat is a major emerging risk. Prolonged heat waves and 
very hot days have become more frequent, especially in lowland regions and major cities 
such as Tashkent. National assessments show that circulatory diseases dominate 
mortality, and heat events further elevate all-cause mortality above seasonal baselines. 
Analyses using biometeorological indices (Humidex and Apparent Temperature) indicate 
a shift from “moderate” summer discomfort in the mid-20th century to severe or dangerous 
conditions in many lowland districts, particularly near irrigated zones and reservoirs. The 
most vulnerable groups include older adults, infants, and young children, people wi th 
cardio-respiratory diseases, outdoor workers, and residents of dense urban areas. Annex 
II summarizes the underlying health statistics and the Humidex/AT diagnostics. 
 
c. Floods, mudflows, and landslides.  Uzbekistan faces increasing hydrometeorological 
extremes, especially floods, mudflows, and landslides in mountainous and foothill zones. 
Debris flows (“sel”) in steep channels can reach high velocities and carry large boulder s, 
damaging infrastructure, cropland, and transport corridors within minutes. Nat ional 
inventories identify hundreds of active torrent catchments and close to a thousand 
exposed economic and social assets. Most events occur in spring to early summer and 
are triggered by intense rainfall, rapid snowmelt, accelerated glacier melt, and in some

B. Projections and Scenarios 
 
8. Uzbekistan’s Fourth National Communication uses a 16 -model CMIP5 ensemble 
(RCP2.6/4.5/8.5)19 refined to station scale to assess climate projections. Model performance i s 
high for temperature and acceptable for precipitation. Detailed station-level statistics and scenario 
plots are provided in Annex II. 
 
a. Temperature.  All scenarios show robust warming. By late century (2080 –2099), annual 
mean temperature is projected to increase by about +1.3°C under RCP2.6, ~+2.5 –3°C 
under RCP4.5, and up to ~+5°C under RCP8.5 relative to 1986 –2005. Warming is the 
strongest in summer and in lowland deserts (e.g., Karakalpakstan, desert parts of Navoi), 
with smaller but still significant increases in mountain zones. Near-ter m summer 
anomalies are already on the order of +1–2°C and intensify in high-emission scenarios. 
 
b. Precipitation and snow.  Annual precipitation changes are modest (generally within 
±5%), but seasonal shifts are substantial. In key runoff-formation zones of the Syr Darya 
and Amu Darya, winter and spring precipitation tend to increase while summer 
precipitation declines, leading to more water in the cool season and higher drought risk in 
summer. In mountain basins (e.g., Chirchik –Ahangaran), warming shortens the frost 
season and reduces snow cover duration, contributing to earlier snowmelt and changes 
in runoff timing. 
 
c. Water management.  These projections imply higher summer heat stress and irrigation 
demand, increased intra-annual variability of water supply, and higher probabilities of 
droughts, flash floods, and debris flows. They underscore the need to integrate climate -
informed design values into storage, conveyance, and flood-protection infrastructure, and 
to strengthen observation networks and seasonal planning. Full scenario details (tim e 
slices, regional breakdowns, and figures) are provided in Annex II.

A. Regional Coordination for Water Resources in Central Asia 
 
13. Central Asia faces accelerating climate risks that no country can manage alon e. Regional 
cooperation therefore hinges on a complementary ecosystem of platforms. The Central Asia 
Regional Economic Cooperation (CAREC) Program serves as the main coordination chann el, 
aligning policies and investments around its 2025–2027 Climate Change Action Plan. This covers 
climate-risk preparedness and health, water –energy–food nexus, low-carbon growth, and a 
shared regional climate platform. The International Fund for Saving the Aral Sea (IFAS), created 
by five Central Asian states in 1993, provides the interstate framework for transboundary water 
governance and climate adaptation in the Aral Sea basin. The Regional Environmental Centre for 
Central Asia complements these efforts as an independent convener, linking go vernments, 
NGOs, and stakeholders to translate evidence and dialogue into practical actions for 
environmental sustainability. These initiatives jointly: 
 
i. coordinate regional policy, standards, and project pipelines for adaptation and mitigation; 
ii. tackle transboundary water and climate risks through shared governance and joint 
planning; 
iii. mobilize finance and partnerships for low-carbon, climate-resilient growth; and 
iv. Build common knowledge base —data, assessments, and capacity for risk-informed 
decisions. 
 
14. This architecture enables Central Asia to speak with a stronger regional voice, scale wha t 
works across borders, and connect local priorities to global climate processes. Because cl imate 
impacts in Central Asia are mediated largely through water —affecting energy, agriculture, and 
health, transboundary water governance is the most mature expression of regional cooperation.

18. Control of water resources in the Republic of Uzbekistan is implemented through a tiered 
system of regulation, planning, and enforcement established by Cabinet Resolution No . 82 27 
(2013, as amended). The MoWR and the Ministry of Mining Industry and Geo logy for surface 
waters and groundwater respectively serve as competent authorities. These ministries set annual 
and seasonal abstraction limits prioritizing drinking, medical, and municipal need s based on 
hydrological forecasts from the Uzhydromet.  
 
19. The Ministry of Water Resources (MoWR) is responsible for reservoir and canal 
operations and dam safety actions before and during floods such as pre-emptive drawdowns per 
rule curves, gated releases, 24/7 patrols of critical structures, debris management, and r apid

ministry also leads coordination with line ministries and local authorities. The division between 
the two ministries ensures Uzhydromet forecasts are translated into operational measures by the 
MoWR while emergency protection of people and assets is directed by the MES.

strong winds, dust/sandstorms, heat and cold waves, thunderstorms, hail, snow, fog, floods, flash

authoritative data and alerts to the State Emergency Prevention and Response System (SEPRS)

C. Existing Gaps & Challenges 
 
53. Despite major progress, the CIEWS –SEPRS chain remains partially “impact -to-action.” 
Early warnings are timely but not yet anchored to binding, cross-ministerial trigger matrices that 
automatically translate forecast thresholds into MoWR reservoir drawdowns, canal operations, 
and CropAgro rotation/sowing adjustments. Sector bulletins are ad hoc (no fixed  cadence or 
SLAs), and MES coordination is largely incident-driven, compounded by the absence of a unified, 
continuously updated multi-hazard dashboard with shared watch –warning levels across 
Uzhydromet, MES, MoWR and MoA. Observation and communications redundancy is uneven  
(power/backhaul gaps, radar coverage limits), and operations and maintenance/calibration 
budgets are not sustainably ring-fenced. Data standards and identifiers are fragmented  across 
platforms (Suv hisobi, CropAgro, pump meter and cadastre systems), with mete ring roll-out and 
anti-tamper enforcement still incomplete.  
 
54. In the absence of meters, satellite/normative fallbacks create accuracy and disput e-
resolution risks. Last mile conversion of alerts into farm instructions relies heavily on WSS/WUAs 
with variable capacity, digital access, and inclusivity (languages, disability frien dly formats, 
USSD/IVR uptake). Agricultural climate services are duplicated across actors, while capacity in 
impact modeling, GIS, and risk communication at regional/local levels is thin, and turnover rate is 
high. Forecast-based financing and pre-agreed contingency measures (seed/fodder pre-
positioning, protective irrigation, labor/heat protocols) are nascent, and legal/standard operating 
procedure linkages among PKM-82 (water use), No.330 (Agroplatforma), PD-183 (water 
digitalization) and SEPRS procedures need tightening to embed accountabilities, audit trails, and 
appeal pathways. Cross-border flood/drought procedures exist but are not yet fully 
operationalized into routine joint exercises and shared, near real-time basin views.

C. Heat Impact  
Rising heat is a direct public health hazard in Uzbekistan and a powerful amplifier of pre-existing 
risks. Heat acts both directly by causing heat exhaustion and heatstroke and indirectly  by 
stressing the cardiovascular and respiratory systems, worsening air-quality episod es (e.g., 
dust/sandstorms), degrading drinking-water safety, and shifting the ecology of infectious 
diseases. National assessments show that circulatory diseases already dominate mortality, with 
heat events compounding this burden. During very hot months all-cause mortalit y rises above 
seasonal baselines in most regions. July fatalities during recent hot years have exceeded  April–
June averages (e.g., +20% in Khorezm, +16% in Tashkent city). Prolonged heat waves ( multi-
day anomalies ≥ +5 °C above normal) have become more frequent (e.g., large upticks observed 
in Tashkent), and even modest temperature increments (≈ 1–2 °C) are associated with 10–13% 
increases in acute intestinal infections, reflecting the sensitivity of water and food safety to heat. 
 
Two complementary biometeorological indicators underpin heat-risk communication and 
planning. Humidex describes perceived heat load for a person in shade and calm air (temperature 
+ humidity). Values of 40 –45 signal “great discomfort” (avoid exertion), and  ≥ 45  indicate 
dangerous conditions with heat-stroke risk. Apparent Temperature (AT) extends thi s to sunny, 
open-air exposure (add wind and solar radiation), with AT ≥ 42 °C  denoting dangerous stress. 
Observations across 50 stations show a shift from mid-20th- century “moderate” July discomfort 
to severe discomfort in many lowland districts today (e.g., lower Amu Darya, Khorezm, Bukhara, 
Syrdarya, Namangan, Andijan). Scenario analyses project further deterioration . By 2080 –2099 
under high emissions (RCP8.5), humidex may exceed 45 over much of the lowlands—conditions 
dangerous even at rest —while AT commonly enters the “dangerous” category across summer 
months, with the highest stress near irrigated zones and reservoirs. 
 
Health impacts are uneven. Most vulnerable are older adults (≥ 65 y), infants and young children, 
people with cardiovascular/respiratory disease, those with limited access to cool indoor 
environments or safe water, outdoor workers (agriculture, construction, road),  and residents of 
dense urban areas where heat is intensified by built surfaces and waste heat. Analyses using an

social losses. National hydrometeorological analysis identifies 709 mudflow-active watercourses 
covering up to 53,770 km², or around 12% of the national territory, with nearly 900 economic and 
social assets located in mudflow-prone zones. 
 
Recent national data show a clear rise in recorded mudflow and flood events during the last five 
years. Between 2019 and 2023, at least 482 mudflow and flood events were recorded across the 
country, increasing from 51 events in 2019 to 97 in 2020, 124 in 2021, and peaking  at 161 in 
2022, before declining to at least 49 events in 2023. This confirms that the eastern and southern 
regions of Uzbekistan are among the most exposed areas. In approximately 61% of recor ded 
cases, mudflows cause material or social damage. Spatially, impacts are concentrated in the east 
and south: Surkhandarya recorded the highest five-year total (167), followed by Namangan (82), 
Fergana (79), Tashkent (45), Jizzakh (41), Kashkadarya (32), Samarkand (19), Navoi  (14), and 
Andijan (3). The spike in 2022 aligns with wetter-than-usual conditions and is consi stent with 
emerging climate-risk signals in the region.

Temperatures have risen by nearly three times the global average over recent decades,  with 
increases of up to 2.5°C in the Aral Sea region. Droughts have become more frequent,  with six

E. Climate Projections  
Uzbekistan’s Fourth National Communication 41 develops regional climate scenarios from a 16-
model CMIP5 multi-model ensemble (RCP2.6/4.5/8.5), refined to station scale usi ng diagnostic 
synchronicity methods against CRU-TS3.24 and national observations. Model skill is very high 
for monthly air temperature (typical station- month correlations ≈0.88– 0.99) and acceptable for 
precipitation, reflecting global practice. Projections are presented for four time slices: 2020–2039, 
2040–2059, 2060–2079, and 2080–2099.

of the frost/snow season. 
 
F. Green Transition  
Uzbekistan has achieved dynamic economic growth, reaching a GDP of USD115 bil lion in 2024 
with a 6.5% annual increase fueled by structural reforms and rising foreign investmen t. The 
country leads Central Asian growth forecasts, supported by an expanding services sector, robust 
exports, and significant progress in diversification. Policy transformation is anchored in the 
Uzbekistan2030 Strategy and the Strategy for Transition to Green Economy (2019–2030), which 
set ambitious renewable energy, forestry, and water 
management targets. Key initiatives include 
expanding green energy to 40% of supply, scaling climate-smart agriculture, and modernizi ng 
irrigation across 4.3 million hectares. 
 
Governance reforms have established the National Committee of Ecology and Climate C hange 
(NCECC) as the central authority, backed by specialized agencies for climate m onitoring and 
adaptation, forestry conservation, waste management, and climate research. Real-time cl imate 
monitoring and enhanced multi-hazard early warning systems now cover all ma jor regions, 
improving disaster risk management. 
 
Flagship projects such as the Green Climate Fund early warning initiative amounting to USD 9.9 
million to modernize hydro-meteorological networks and integrate climate data  into decision-
making. In November of 2025, Uzbekistan’s updated Nationally Determined Contribution commits 
to a 50% reduction in GHG emissions per GDP unit by 2035. This is r einforced by high-level 
national programs, strong institutional coordination, and significant international sup port. 
Designating 2025 as the "Year of Environmental Protection and Green Economy" signal s 
deepening national resolve for sustainable development and biodiversity protection. 
 
Reforms have strengthened the early warning system, but critical gaps persist. These i nclude: 
uneven last-mile alerting and sensor coverage in remote/high-risk areas, limited pow er and 
communications redundancy, non-harmonized data standards between NCECC, MES  and 
Uzhydromet, underfunded operations and maintenance and system upgrades, capacity and 
retention gaps at subnational level, incomplete inclusion of vulnerable groups in warning delivery, 
and partial cross-border data exchange and joint procedures for transboundary floods  and 
droughts.
